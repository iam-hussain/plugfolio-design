Why they look like this

No images and no tracking pixel

Mail clients block remote images by default, so a design that leans on them arrives broken for most readers. Worse, a blocked pixel in a security email is one of the things spam filters score against you — and these three are the emails that must not land in a spam folder.

The link is printed as text

Buttons do not render everywhere, and a person deciding whether to trust a link wants to read where it goes before pressing it. Both, always — one for convenience, one for the reader who is being careful.

Expiry and single-use, every time

“Works once, expires in 24 hours” is what separates a real security email from a forged one in a reader’s mind. It also sets the expectation before the link runs out, rather than after.

A line for the person who didn’t ask

Every one of these can reach someone whose address a stranger typed. That reader needs to know that ignoring it is safe and sufficient — and the reset email has to read sensibly for them too, because /forgot never reveals whether an account exists.

A reply-to that reaches a person

These are the emails people reply to when they are locked out and frightened. no-reply@ on an account-recovery email tells someone with no other route in that there is no route in.

System fonts, inline styles, tables in production

A webfont is a remote request most mail clients refuse. The rendering above uses the product’s type so the copy can be judged in context; the shipped template is table-based HTML with inline styles and a system stack.
